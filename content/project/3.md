# Binding

## Introduction

**Students will now create a “book,” assembling a collection of texts from their previous work into a cohesive whole—responding via their curation/selections, in writing, and with their design itself.**

The goal of this project is to apply the skills you have now developed across *multiple* web pages—creating your first *website*. This “book” will include a homepage (its cover), navigation (table of contents), individual pages for each text (chapters), and your introduction (a colophon). It will house your texts and allow a reader to move between them all. The final website will be responsive for screen ~~and print~~ layouts across all its pages.

## Develop the System

<div class="body">

Based on feedback, you will now narrow and then build out the foundation across all your pages—adding your additional two texts, incorporating them within the design’s overall system. You will now also *progressively-enhance* your layout for larger breakpoints, and add functional navigational and interface elements throughout.

The landing/cover page should include an introduction (that *you* write), as well as a title for your reader, and a brief [colophon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colophon_(publishing)). From this landing page, you should be able to navigate to the other texts’ pages, and vice-versa—exploring typographic concepts for this navigation.

Your three sketch *direction* subpages will be replaced with pages for your three texts. Each text’s page should still be *unique*—with the semantic elements and styles needed and reflecting their individual content. We should be able to easily tell them apart. But they should balance their individual expression with a cohesive whole, across your website—three *flavors* of the same *dish*. Consider the entire experience, moving through the pages—as a user will—as well as the typography of the individual texts themselves. Your design should be an umbrella across them all, while still allowing expression within each text.

You will also each check in with [your partner](2.md#your-partners) from *Spread*—now, by creating a [GitHub *Issue*](https://docs.github.com/en/issues/tracking-your-work-with-issues/creating-an-issue) in their repo (as we did in the first projects). Use this to help them improve their code—and also offer comments/feedback/encouragement on their design.

## Notes on Format

### “New for 3”

- As we’ve mentioned—we want everyone to see each other’s work. (And it’s more fun when we’re all together!) Practically, this will spread the critiques over our last two class sessions. Half will go on December 3; the other half will go on December 10, our last class.

- In the interest of fairness, *everyone’s* projects are due together on the *first* day, December 3. You should be prepared to and expect to present your work then.

- We will give you the random critique order at the start of class that day, not before. We won’t be taking requests or volunteers for which day you go. It’s random! And as equitable as we can be.

- Since everyone’s projects are due on the first day, you are *not* to work on your project after the start of class on the 3rd—even if you don’t end up going that day. If you draw into the second day, we don’t want to see *any* revisions/commits to your repo, etc.

- *Any* efforts after this point will be seen as you seeking an advantage over your peers, and that is quick way to fail the project (and likely, the class). “Pencils down” is December 3. You’re done!

- If your crit is on the first day, you are still expected to be in class on the second! Your classmates sat through your work; you should do the same for them. Missing the last class would also reflect poorly on your own grade.

- We will be “driving” for these—opening your work ourselves, scrolling around, “popping the hood” into your code. Prepare to talk about your work *while* we’re doing this, not in some predetermined sequence.

- We’ll again go through each project in turn—presenting from the *live, public URL* ~~from one of your computers, joining Zoom for the projector and recording.~~ on our machine. We’ll still have you join the Zoom, from yours, for recording your explanations.

- We’ll give you some time at the start to introduce us to your work—but think of this more as a discussion, less as a presentation!

### “The Usual”

- [Submit](https://forms.gle/w7Y1GmHEDZeY53w39) your work, as we have before. Be sure to check on other devices, as we’ll be opening it in on ours!

- Be prepared to go when it is your turn!

- Help us improve on our <nobr>A/V</nobr> consistency—microphone on, camera on.

- Still no decks, Slides, Figma, or scripts.

- Introduce us to your selected readings, design concept, and its execution. *Briefly* give us any context we need.

- We’ll shift into a discussion and critique of your project, asking about your work—and you should be prepared to answer and explain any design and implementation detail/rationale in it.

- Per our [community agreement](../syllabus.md#our-community) (and courtesy), the presenting student “has the floor.” Everyone else will close their laptops and turn off their phones—and no one should <nobr>come-and-go</nobr> from the room during someone else’s turn.

- As before, we will evaluate your projects on your explanation of the work, the work itself, and its technical implementation—based on [our expectations](#our-expectations) (review these!), aesthetics, usability, quality, and your engagement.

- We’ll average these scores for your overall project grade, [per CD letter standards](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1u358io8doX_SVVMGqIM_oH5V0OIccneYu4Ww-uE55QM/edit?tab=t.0#heading=h.mszve56er8ls), which we will share again on Slack—along with your overall course grade for the semester.
